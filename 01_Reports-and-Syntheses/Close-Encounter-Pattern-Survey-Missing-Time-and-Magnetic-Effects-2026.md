# Close-Encounter Pattern Survey: Missing Time and Magnetic Effects (2026)

Literature survey compiled 2026-11-10. Scope: aggregate patterns in close-range UFO/UAP reports that could constrain craft physics, taken under the project's working premise. Individual cases appear only as examples.

## Bottom line

1. **There is no published distance law for missing time.** I found no catalogue or study that tabulates missing time against distance, and none that gives a rate of missing time among close encounters versus distant sightings. Every dataset that contains missing time is selected for it.
2. **The best-quantified pattern is vehicle interference**, from Rodeghier's 441-event catalogue (1981). It gives effect types, a median object distance of about 200 ft, durations, and co-occurrence statistics. It did not establish that severity depends on distance, and its data are almost entirely secondhand.
3. **Missing time and EM effects do not visibly travel together.** In Schuessler's 356-case physiological catalogue, time loss (75 cases) and vehicle EM effects (77 cases) are each about 21 to 22%, but my tally finds vehicle interference in only about 15% of the time-loss cases, no higher than the catalogue-wide rate. Rodeghier's vehicle catalogue does not list time loss as an effect category at all.
4. **Missing time is strongly tied to era and to retrieval method.** In the Schuessler catalogue, time-loss language appears in roughly 10% of 1950s to 1960s cases and roughly 40% of 1970s to 1980s cases (TALLY). Most detailed accounts in the main samples were obtained under hypnosis. The classic gap (Hill case) was established with investigators about two months after the event.
5. **Instrumented magnetic measurements are few and weak.** Hessdalen 1984 recorded sub-10-gamma pulsations with a 4-of-10 coincidence that its own author says may be chance. The Condon project's remanent-magnetism test on two cars was null; two Australian tests were null; one British test was positive. No instrument record exists for any missing-time event that I could find.
6. **Evidence quality is low throughout.** The numbers below describe the content of reports and of investigator-built catalogues. They are not measurements of events.

### A.2 Answers to the questions

1. **Close versus distant, threshold distance.** Not answerable from the literature. No source gives a denominator. The only quasi-rate is A1, and that catalogue is selected for bodily effects. The DIA document adopts a working distance of tens of metres and exposure under ten minutes for injuries (V-T), which is an assumption for its analysis and not a measured threshold.
2. **Durations.** Typically 1 to 2 hours (A4, A11). Short gaps of 15 to 45 minutes exist. Multi-day absences are rare outliers.
3. **What is reported.** Overwhelmingly a memory gap discovered against outside clocks (arrival time, a clock at home). A timepiece anomaly accompanies a minority: about 3% of the Schuessler catalogue and at most about 15% of its time-loss cases (A2, A5). When a timepiece is mentioned it is most often stopped, sometimes slow, almost never fast. Body-clock evidence is nearly absent. I found one case (Chile 1977, in A5) of several days' beard growth during an absence of about fifteen minutes, which points the opposite way to a slow-time region. I found no hunger or menstrual-timing data in any catalogue.
4. **Spontaneous or reconstructed.** No source gives a clean proportion for when the gap was first noticed. Proxies: 74 of 104 detailed narratives from hypnosis (A9); 18 of 43 British accounts spontaneous (A10); 8 of 10 Harvard abductees used hypnosis and none in the 2002 sample had continuous memory (A13); the Hill gap was established weeks later (A14). A widely repeated claim that missing time rises from about 70% in conscious-recall cases to about 95% under hypnosis (attributed to Bullard) surfaced only in a search summary of a blog and could not be traced. **UNV; do not use.**
5. **Onset.** Randles's "Oz Factor" (traffic vanishing, birdsong stopping, isolation) is an investigator impression with no percentage attached (SEC, via Bryan). Bullard's 2023 summary describes a vacuum-like silence descending on a motorist as the car stops (V-T: [Zenodo PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/10588354/files/Reliability_III-2_Bullard.pdf)). In Randles's sample 55% began with a UFO and 30% with a bright light (SEC). Paralysis appears in 21% of the Schuessler catalogue (A1) and 3% of vehicle-interference events (B2). Nothing I found quantifies whether onset is sharp in space. The Harvard group notes that tingling, buzzing, flashing lights and a levitation feeling are ordinary sleep-paralysis features (V-T).
6. **Shared missing time.** About a quarter of Bullard's cases involve two to seven people taken together (A7), while 76% are single-witness (A8). Independent documentation (separately interviewed witnesses, timepieces compared at the time) was not reported in any aggregate source. The Sturrock panel made the same complaint about physiological cases generally: an unaffected independent witness is usually missing (V-F).

### B.3 Answers to the questions

1. **Types.** Engine stalls dominate; lights and radio follow (B1). Compass effects are a small share of aircraft cases (B12). Timepiece effects are rare (A5). Power outages have no verified link (Condon). Magnetised objects are single anecdotes (B15).
2. **Quantities.** Distances cluster at tens to a few hundred feet (B2). Fall-off with distance is contested: Johnson says stalls become likelier with proximity (B6); Rodeghier could not show severity depends on distance (B5). Self-restart is uncommon, 7 to 9% (B3). Diesel is under 1% (B7), with an obvious confound: diesel passenger vehicles were rare in the sampled fleets. No compass deflection angles or field strengths were measured at any vehicle event.
3. **Instrumented results.** See B.2. Summary: a handful of nanotesla-scale coincidences at kilometre range, and nulls or untested opportunities everywhere else.
4. **Correlation with missing time or physiological effects.** Within vehicle events, bodily effects cluster with light beams, loss of vehicle control and chasing (B4). Across the physiological catalogue, time loss does not cluster with vehicle interference (A2).

### D.3 Known problems with the data

- **Hypnosis and confabulation.** See D.1. The foundational book rested on seven hypnotised subjects (A15).
- **Cultural contamination.** Bullard's later comparison used 52 cases for 1966 to 1977, 131 for 1978 to 1986 and 254 for 1987 to 1996 (SEC: [Substack summary](https://cassiopaea.substack.com/p/alien-abduction-part-1a)); my tally in A3 shows time-loss wording quadrupling after the 1960s. Entity types differ by country (12% "Grays" in Britain versus 73% in the US, Randles via Bryan, SEC), which is what an investigator or media effect predicts. Engine-stopping rays were a fiction and rumour motif decades before 1947 (Kottmeyer, V-F).
- **Selection.** Every catalogue is selected on the effect it counts. None reports how many close approaches had no effect. Rodeghier's own summary notes that close approaches without interference exist.
- **Secondhand data.** B8. Hendry found over twenty EM cases mundane on investigation (SEC via Kottmeyer). The move to solid-state ignition in the 1970s changes the susceptible population.
- **Single-witness dominance.** 50% of vehicle events (B2) and 76% of abductions (A8).
- **The DIA document.** Its author field is redacted in the released copy; attribution to Christopher Green rests on secondary sources. It attributes injuries mainly to radio-frequency and microwave exposure, not to magnetic fields, and treats the Schuessler catalogue as given (V-T).

**Reading of the table.** On present evidence H-cognitive accounts for the most patterns and is contradicted mainly by group cases and by physical after-effects that it does not address. H-field has real support for a short-range, ignition-specific, transient disturbance to vehicles, but the specifically magnetic version is contradicted by the car-body tests, and its predicted link to missing time is absent. H-bubble has almost no positive support: three slow-watch anecdotes and an impressionistic sharp onset, against the basic structure of the reports (amnesia with normal elapsed time). The data are too poor to exclude any of the three; they are good enough to say which predictions have been looked for and failed.

## Independent verification and corrections (2026-11-10)

A second pass by the coordinating reviewer re-opened the load-bearing sources.

**Confirmed against the source pages**

- Rodeghier chapters 6 and 9: 441 events, median distance about 200 ft, 35% within 100 ft, physiological effect in 98 events (heat 29, paralysis 14), about half single-witness, no time-loss category, severity-versus-distance not established, and the interference counts in B1.
- DIA document Appendix A: 356 cases; apparent abductions 129; vehicle EM effects 77; paralysis 75; perceived time loss 75.
- Condon chapter conclusion and the 106 / 45 EM-case counts. The source for those counts is Hall's *The UFO Evidence* (NICAP, 1964).

**Corrections**

- B9: the Condon bench thresholds are for ignition components on a bench, not a running engine. The 20 kG figure is an occasional spark interruption in the steel-cased coil, not a stop point.
- B10: Condon case 12 was a reported loss of acceleration with radio and instrument faults, not a stall. Case 39 is the one reported stoppage.

**Independent recount of the Schuessler catalogue** (own parser, 354 of 356 cases recovered)

| Quantity | Recount | Survey figure |
|---|---|---|
| "Time loss" in the effects list | 78 cases | 75 (DIA table) |
| 1950s | 3 of 65 (5%) | 9% |
| 1960s | 5 of 59 (8%) | 12% |
| 1970s | 51 of 153 (33%) | 38% |
| 1980s | 16 of 53 (30%) | 40% |
| Vehicle EM wording in time-loss cases | 13 of 78 (17%) | 15% |
| Vehicle EM wording in all cases | 92 of 354 (26%) | 22% |
| "Abduction" wording in time-loss cases | 63 of 78 (81%) | 82% |
| "Abduction" wording in all cases | 144 of 354 (41%) | — |

The recount uses stricter wording than the survey's tally, so its percentages are a little lower. The patterns are the same: time loss rises several-fold after 1970, travels with the abduction label, and is not associated with vehicle interference.

**One interpretive correction.** The hypothesis table scores "the witness has a memory gap" against H-bubble on the grounds that a slow-time region gives continuous memory. That distinction cannot be drawn from these catalogues. A person inside a slow-time region would have a short continuous experience and then find outside clocks ahead, and would describe that as missing time. The discriminating datum is whether the witness's own watch agreed with outside clocks afterwards. The catalogue records it in a handful of cases only (slow by 15 minutes; slow by 10 hours; "lost time"; a car clock 5.5 hours off; several stopped). H-bubble is therefore untested on its sharpest prediction rather than contradicted by it. Its other predictions (dependence on proximity, independence of era and of the abduction label) are the ones the data count against.

## Method notes

- Sources were opened with a web fetch tool. PDFs it could not parse were converted to text and read directly; those are the V-T items.
- TALLY figures come from splitting the Schuessler OCR text into 317 case blocks (the catalogue has 356 cases; some blocks merge two) and matching keywords. The paralysis count from this method (75) matches the DIA table exactly; the time-loss count (87) is higher than the DIA table's 75 because I included memory-loss and amnesia wording. Treat all TALLY percentages as approximate.
- No PDFs were stored in this repository.
